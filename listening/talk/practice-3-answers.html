Talk 1 — The Influence of Jazz on American Culture (Art & Music)

話者：教授 ｜ StandardSpeaker: Professor | Standard

スクリプトを見る（シグナルワードをハイライト付き）View transcript (signal words highlighted)
Today we're going to look at how jazz music shaped American culture in the twentieth century. Jazz originated in the African American communities of New Orleans in the late 1800s, blending elements of blues, ragtime, and European harmonies.

What made jazz truly revolutionary was its emphasis on improvisation — musicians didn't just play written notes; they created new melodies on the spot, responding to each other in real time.

By the 1920s, jazz had spread across the country and became the soundtrack of what we call the Jazz Age. It wasn't just music — it influenced fashion, language, and social attitudes. Jazz clubs became spaces where racial barriers were, at least partially, broken down, as people of different backgrounds came together to enjoy the music.

Artists like Louis Armstrong and Duke Ellington became cultural icons whose influence extended far beyond music. Jazz also laid the groundwork for later genres, including rhythm and blues, rock and roll, and hip-hop. In many ways, jazz represents the spirit of American creativity — the idea that something beautiful can emerge from collaboration, spontaneity, and the willingness to take risks.

Talk 2 — How Glaciers Shape Landscapes (Physical Science)

話者：教授 ｜ HarderSpeaker: Professor | Harder

スクリプトを見る（シグナルワードをハイライト付き）View transcript (signal words highlighted)
Let's talk about one of the most powerful forces in shaping the Earth's surface — glaciers. A glacier is essentially a massive body of ice that forms over centuries as layers of snow compact and recrystallize. But glaciers are not just static ice formations; they move, and it is this movement that makes them such effective agents of landscape change.

Glaciers move through a combination of two processes. The first is internal deformation, where the weight of the ice causes it to slowly flow, almost like extremely thick liquid. The second process is basal sliding, where a thin layer of meltwater at the base of the glacier allows it to slide over the bedrock beneath.

As glaciers advance, they carry with them enormous amounts of rock and sediment, scraping and grinding the land beneath them. This process, called glacial erosion, creates distinctive landforms. For example, U-shaped valleys are carved by glaciers moving through mountain regions, in contrast to the V-shaped valleys created by rivers. Glaciers also create features called moraines, which are ridges of debris deposited along the edges and at the front of the glacier.

When a glacier retreats, it often leaves behind deep depressions that fill with water, forming glacial lakes. The Great Lakes of North America are perhaps the most famous example of lakes formed by glacial activity.

Scientists study glaciers not only to understand past geological events but also because glaciers serve as sensitive indicators of climate change. As global temperatures rise, glaciers around the world are retreating at an accelerating rate.
