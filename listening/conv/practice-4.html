Conversation 1

Listen to a conversation.
Setting: Residence Hall Office — Student (male) + Residence Advisor (female)
Student: Hi, I need to talk to someone about the noise in my building. My neighbor plays loud music almost every night until two AM.
Advisor: I'm sorry to hear that. Have you tried talking to your neighbor directly about it?
Student: I did, twice actually, but nothing has changed. Last night was the worst. I have an exam tomorrow and I couldn't study at all.
Advisor: I understand your frustration. In cases like this, the first step is to file a formal noise complaint. I can help you fill out the form right now.
Student: Will that actually make a difference? I don't want to make things awkward between us.
Advisor: The complaint is handled confidentially. Your neighbor won't know who filed it. After we receive the complaint, we'll issue a written warning. If the behavior continues, there are further consequences, including possible room reassignment.
Student: Okay, that sounds fair. Can I also request a temporary room change while this is being resolved? I really need to sleep properly this week.
Advisor: Absolutely. We have a few empty rooms on the third floor. I can arrange a temporary move for you starting tonight.

Q1. Why does the student visit the residence advisor?

A) To request a permanent room transfer to another building.
B) To complain about a noisy neighbor in his building.
C) To report a broken lock on his door.
D) To ask about extending the quiet hours policy.

Q2. What does the advisor offer to do for the student?

A) Speak directly to the noisy neighbor on his behalf.
B) Move him to a different building permanently.
C) Help file a complaint and arrange a temporary room change.
D) Provide him with noise-canceling headphones to use.

Conversation 2

Listen to a conversation.
Setting: Career Services Center — Student (female) + Career Counselor (male)
Student: Hi, I'm looking for help with my resume. I'm applying for a summer internship at a marketing firm, and I want to make sure my resume stands out.
Counselor: Great, let me take a look at what you have so far. Do you have a copy with you?
Student: Yes, here it is. I'm worried that I don't have enough work experience since I've mostly done volunteer work and class projects.
Counselor: Actually, that's perfectly fine for an internship application. Employers know students may not have extensive professional experience. What matters is how you present your skills and accomplishments.
Student: So I should focus more on what I learned from those experiences?
Counselor: Exactly. For example, your volunteer work at the community center — instead of just listing it, describe the specific campaign you organized and the results you achieved. Use numbers whenever possible.
Student: That makes sense. Should I also include my social media project from my marketing class?
Counselor: Definitely. That's directly relevant to the position. I'd also recommend adding a skills section at the top highlighting your proficiency in tools like Canva and Google Analytics.

Q3. What does the counselor imply about the student's experience?

A) Her volunteer work and class projects are sufficient for an internship.
B) She should get a part-time job before applying.
C) She cannot qualify without professional work experience.
D) She needs a recommendation letter from her professor.

Q4. What can be inferred about how the student will revise her resume?

A) She will apply to a different company with fewer requirements.
B) She will get more work experience before applying.
C) She will remove the volunteer work from the resume.
D) She will add specific details about her skills and accomplishments.
