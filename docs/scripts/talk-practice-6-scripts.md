========================================
TALK 1 SCRIPT (talk1.mp3)
========================================
[MAN] Today we're going to look at one of the most important innovations in the history of Western art — the development of linear perspective during the Renaissance. Before the fifteenth century, European paintings appeared flat. Figures were often shown the same size regardless of their position in the scene. This changed dramatically when the Italian architect Filippo Brunelleschi conducted a series of experiments around 1415, demonstrating how three-dimensional space could be represented on a two-dimensional surface using mathematical principles. Shortly after, the painter Masaccio applied these ideas in his famous fresco The Holy Trinity, creating a remarkably convincing illusion of depth. The key technique involves establishing a single vanishing point on the horizon line, toward which all parallel lines in the scene converge. This gave artists the ability to create realistic spatial relationships between objects and figures. The impact was enormous — perspective became the foundation of European painting for the next five hundred years.

TALK 2 SCRIPT (talk2.mp3)
========================================
[WOMAN] I'd like to discuss a fascinating question that has occupied linguists and psychologists for decades: does the language you speak affect the way you think? This idea is known as the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis, named after the linguists Edward Sapir and Benjamin Lee Whorf. Now, there are two versions of this hypothesis. The strong version claims that language determines thought — meaning you literally cannot think about concepts that your language has no words for. Most modern researchers reject this extreme view. However, the weak version, known as linguistic relativity, suggests that language influences thought and perception without completely determining them. And there is growing evidence to support this weaker claim. Consider color perception, for example. The Russian language has separate basic words for light blue and dark blue, treating them as fundamentally different colors. Studies have shown that Russian speakers can actually distinguish between shades of blue faster than English speakers, who use one word for both. Another striking example comes from the Kuuk Thaayorre people of Australia, who do not use words like left and right. Instead, they describe spatial relationships using cardinal directions — north, south, east, and west. Research has found that speakers of this language have an extraordinary sense of orientation, always knowing which direction they are facing, even in unfamiliar environments. These examples suggest that while language does not imprison our thoughts, it does provide a kind of lens through which we interpret the world. The words available to us can highlight certain distinctions and make them easier to process, shaping our everyday experience in subtle but measurable ways.
