The Discovery of Penicillin

Before the twentieth century, even minor bacterial infections could prove fatal. A simple cut or a common throat infection sometimes led to death because physicians had no reliable way to stop bacteria from spreading inside the body. The discovery of penicillin fundamentally changed medicine and saved countless lives during the decades that followed.

In 1928, the Scottish bacteriologist Alexander Fleming returned to his London laboratory after a vacation and noticed something unusual in one of his petri dishes. A mold had accidentally contaminated a culture of Staphylococcus bacteria, and the area around the mold was completely clear of growth. Fleming correctly concluded that the mold, later identified as Penicillium notatum, was releasing a substance that inhibited bacterial growth. He named this substance penicillin and published his findings the following year.

Despite its promise, penicillin remained little more than a laboratory curiosity for over a decade. Fleming lacked the resources to produce the substance in a pure, stable form. It was not until the early 1940s that Howard Florey and Ernst Chain, working at Oxford University, developed techniques to isolate and manufacture penicillin in quantities large enough for medical use. During World War II, mass production of penicillin reduced deaths from infected wounds, and the drug soon became the first widely used antibiotic, opening a new era of modern medicine.

How Telescopes Changed Our Understanding of the Universe

Before the invention of the telescope, humans understood the universe through naked-eye observation. Ancient astronomers tracked planets and stars with impressive accuracy, but their view of the cosmos was fundamentally limited. The development of optical telescopes in the early seventeenth century transformed astronomy from a practice based on visible patterns into a science capable of revealing previously invisible details about celestial objects.

The Italian scientist Galileo Galilei was among the first to use a telescope for systematic astronomical observation. In 1610, he discovered four moons orbiting Jupiter, providing strong evidence that not everything in the sky revolved around the Earth. This observation directly challenged the geocentric model that had dominated European thought for over a thousand years. Galileo also observed the phases of Venus and the rough, cratered surface of the Moon, further demonstrating that celestial bodies were physical objects subject to natural laws rather than perfect, unchanging spheres.

In the centuries that followed, telescopes grew dramatically in size and sophistication. Reflecting telescopes, which use mirrors instead of lenses, allowed astronomers to observe fainter and more distant objects. By the twentieth century, radio telescopes opened a new window on the universe by detecting electromagnetic radiation invisible to the human eye. The Hubble Space Telescope has captured images of galaxies billions of light-years away, revealing the vast scale of the universe.
