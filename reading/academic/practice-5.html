The Physics of Sound Waves

Sound is a form of energy that travels through matter as a wave. Unlike light, which can move through empty space, sound requires a medium such as air, water, or solid material to propagate. When an object vibrates, it causes the molecules around it to compress and expand in a repeating pattern, creating what scientists call a longitudinal wave.

The speed at which sound travels depends on the properties of the medium. In general, sound moves fastest through solids, slower through liquids, and slowest through gases. For example, sound travels through steel at approximately 5,960 meters per second, but through air at only about 343 meters per second at room temperature. This difference occurs because molecules in solids are packed more tightly together, allowing vibrations to be transmitted more quickly from one molecule to the next.

Two important characteristics of sound waves are frequency and amplitude. Frequency, measured in hertz, determines the pitch of a sound: higher frequencies produce higher-pitched sounds. Amplitude, on the other hand, determines loudness. A sound wave with greater amplitude carries more energy and is perceived as louder by the human ear. Together, these properties explain why a whisper sounds different from a shout, even when both are produced at the same pitch.

The Impact of Urbanization on Wildlife

As cities expand, natural habitats are increasingly replaced by roads, buildings, and other infrastructure. This process, known as urbanization, presents significant challenges for wildlife. Animals that once occupied forests, wetlands, and grasslands must adapt, relocate, or face declining populations.

A visible effect of urbanization is habitat fragmentation. When continuous natural land is divided by highways or housing developments, animal populations become isolated. This isolation reduces genetic diversity, limits access to food and mates, and makes populations more vulnerable to disease and environmental changes. Studies of suburban amphibians have shown that roads act as barriers, preventing frogs and salamanders from reaching breeding ponds.

However, not all species are negatively affected by urbanization. Animals such as raccoons, pigeons, and certain hawks have proven remarkably successful at exploiting urban environments. These species benefit from abundant food sources, including garbage, and nesting sites provided by buildings and bridges. Ecologists call such animals urban adapters, and their success raises questions about how biodiversity changes rather than simply declines in response to human development.

Researchers are exploring strategies to reduce urbanization's harmful effects on wildlife. Wildlife corridors — strips of natural habitat connecting fragmented areas — allow animals to move between isolated patches. Green rooftops and urban parks also provide small but valuable refuges for birds, insects, and other organisms within cities.
