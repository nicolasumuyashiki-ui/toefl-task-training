The Psychology of Decision Making

Every day, people make hundreds of decisions, from what to eat for breakfast to how to invest their savings. While many assume that human decision making is a purely rational process, research in psychology has shown that it is heavily influenced by cognitive biases. These biases are systematic patterns of thinking that can lead individuals to make judgments that deviate from logic or probability.

One of the most well-studied biases is the anchoring effect. When people are presented with an initial piece of information, known as an anchor, their subsequent estimates tend to stay close to that anchor even when it is completely arbitrary. For example, in a classic experiment, researchers asked participants to estimate the percentage of African countries in the United Nations. Before answering, participants spun a wheel that landed on either 10 or 65. Those who saw the number 10 gave significantly lower estimates than those who saw 65, despite the number being randomly generated.

Another common bias is the availability heuristic, which causes people to overestimate the likelihood of events that are easily recalled from memory. Because dramatic events such as plane crashes receive extensive media coverage, many people believe that air travel is more dangerous than driving, even though statistics show the opposite. Psychologists argue that understanding these biases is the first step toward making better decisions in both personal and professional life.

Plate Tectonics and Mountain Formation

The surface of the Earth may appear stable and unchanging, but it is actually composed of enormous slabs of rock called tectonic plates that are in constant, slow motion. These plates float on a layer of semi-molten rock in the upper mantle known as the asthenosphere. The movement of tectonic plates is driven primarily by convection currents within the mantle, where hotter material rises toward the surface and cooler material sinks back down.

When two tectonic plates collide, the immense pressure can cause the Earth's crust to buckle and fold upward, creating mountain ranges. This process, known as orogeny, is responsible for the formation of some of the world's most prominent mountain chains. The Himalayas, for instance, were formed by the ongoing collision between the Indian Plate and the Eurasian Plate, a process that began approximately 50 million years ago and continues to this day. As a result, the Himalayas are still rising by about five millimeters per year.

Not all mountain formation occurs at convergent boundaries, however. Volcanic mountains, such as Mount Fuji in Japan, form when magma from deep within the Earth erupts through the surface at locations where plates are pulling apart or where one plate slides beneath another. Geologists refer to these zones of intense volcanic activity as subduction zones. The study of plate tectonics has fundamentally transformed our understanding of geological processes, providing a unified framework that explains not only mountain building but also earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, and the distribution of fossils across continents.
